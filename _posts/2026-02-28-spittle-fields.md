Spittle-Fields is a fantasy reimagining of Spitalfields, a district in the London Borough of Tower Hamlets. The name
first appeared in the 12th century, when St. Mary Spital was founded as a priory and hospital to minister to the poor,
sick and infirm ("spital" being a corruption of "hospital").

In the 19th century, Mary Kelly lived and died in Spitalfields. A hundred years later, Iain Sinclair worked there as a 
gardener. In the 1990s, more than 10,000 bodies were exhumed from a mass grave on the site of Spitalfields Market.

<em>Return of the Ripper</em> freely combines historical elements from the Elizabethan era through the Victorian era,
to create a deliberately anachronistic imaginary city. <em>London: The Biography</em> (Peter Ackroyd, 2000) supplied
anecdotal details and historical texture.

![Maps have POTENCY; may yield a wealth of knowledge past imagining if properly divined. William Gull (From Hell).](/assets/images/MapsHavePotencyQuote.png)

The aesthetic of the Spittle-Fields maps is inspired by the 16th-century "Agas" woodcut map of
London<sup><a href="#footnote-1">1</a></sup>, which shows the area as <em>The spitel Fyeld</em>, outside the city wall
and beyond the <em>Busshoppes Gate</em> (Bishopsgate).

The street plans and position of major landmarks are loosely based on 19th-century maps of Spitalfields; many
locations use actual street and place names from that era. My research brought me back to the extant Victorian-era
landmarks: Spitalfields Market, The Ten Bells, Christ Church Spitalfields.

Another inspirational oddity is Denis Severs' House<sup><a href="#footnote-2">2</a></sup>. Severs refurbished and
furnished the ten rooms of his home as dioramas of life in Spitalfields in the 18th--19th centuries. Peter Ackroyd
describes a journey through the house as:
<blockquote>
&hellip;a journey through time; with its small rooms and hidden corridors, its whispered asides and sudden revelations,
it resembles a pilgrimage through life itself.
</blockquote>
