Sewers are a recurring trope in urban fantasy adventures, appearing in some of the earliest published adventure modules,
such as <em>Verbosh</em> (Judges Guild, 1979) and <em>Slave Pits of the Undercity</em> (TSR, 1980)<sup><a href="#footnote-2">2</a></sup>.
The sewer motif persists in Goodman Games' Punjar setting<sup><a href="#footnote-3">3</a></sup>.
The encounter areas from <em>Legend of the Ripper</em>'s Cellar and Sewers level were adapted and incorporated into
<em>Return of the Ripper</em>'s Undercity.

Although sewers do not figure directly in Ripper lore, they are emblematic of the Victorian era. London's sewage
system ranks among the most impressive Victorian engineering achievements. Hidden channels of filth flowing
under London streets also have strong Gothic horror associations, symbolising the Victorians' suppressed anxieties
about morally unclean realities that must not be spoken of in polite society.

For centuries, London's rivers had been used as a dumping ground for refuse, dung and dead things, including the
bodies of people robbed or slain. In 1560, a doctor wrote of the spread of disease along the banks of the river Fleet:
<blockquote>
In its stinking lanes, there died most in London and were soonest inflicted, and were longest continued.
</blockquote>
150 years later, the situation had not improved, inciting the satirical poems of Jonathan Swift<sup><a href="#footnote-4">4</a></sup>:
<blockquote>
Sweepings from butchers’ stalls, dung, guts, and blood,<br />
Drowned puppies, stinking sprats, all drenched in mud,<br />
Dead cats and turnip-tops come tumbling down the flood.
</blockquote>
and Alexander Pope<sup><a href="#footnote-5">5</a></sup>:
<blockquote>
To where Fleet-ditch with disemboguing streams<br />
Rolls the large tribute of dead dogs to Thames.
</blockquote>
In 1732, the city authorities bricked over the river, as if it was a guilty secret that must be hidden from view.

The brick sewers of the Fleet and Walbrook continued to fall short of the needs of the growing city. Things came to a
head in the hot summer of 1858, when the stench of untreated human and industrial waste became intolerable. In Sir
John Tenniel's cartoon in <em>Punch</em>, Death rows along the Thames looking for those responsible for not paying to
clean up the river. Finally, Parliament commissioned the civil engineer Joseph Bazalgette to design a modern sewage
system to provide relief.

My first encounter with London's sewer system in fiction was <em>The Talons of Weng Chiang</em>, a <em>Doctor Who</em>
story by Robert Holmes. Holmes often included Gothic allusions and horror elements in his scripts, and is probably the
person most responsible for my childhood nightmares. The stage is set in Victorian London, complete with sewers and
bricked-up river Fleet. As <em>Doctor Who: the Complete Guide</em> puts it, "groaning under the weight of so much
clichéd Victoriana, <em>Talons</em> emerges as a garish hybrid of science fiction and literary pastiche". While it
has a strong script and great performance from Tom Baker, this serial comes with a content warning for its stereotyped
portrayal of Chinese immigrants and the use of a white actor to portray the villain Li H'sen Chang.

On the big screen, Guy Ritchie's <em>Sherlock Holmes</em> has the eponymous consulting detective and his erstwhile
companion pursue the villainous Lord Blackwood into the sewers beneath the Palace of Westminster. Part of the action
was filmed in Crossness Pumping Station, the Victorian "cathedral of sewage" constructed as part of Bazalgette's
works (1859--65)<sup><a href="#footnote-6">6</a></sup>.

Back on the small screen, in the episode <em>London Underground</em> of the BBC police procedural <em>New Tricks</em>,
the investigation follows the hidden river Fleet in search of a cult making blood sacrifices to the spirit of the river.
This episode taps into the psychogeography of London's underground waterways.

Neil Gaiman's <em>Neverwhere</em> (1996) is a fairy tale that presents the sewers as a magical place, a parallel London called
"London Below" where everything forgotten by the world above ends up<sup><a href="#footnote-7">7</a></sup>.

The idea for the corrupted naïad Peg Powler was dredged from the same sludge as the Nene Hag, the tragic and terrifying
water sprite in Alan Moore's epic, <em>Jerusalem</em> (2016). Her name is from the folklore of the river Tees in northern
England.
